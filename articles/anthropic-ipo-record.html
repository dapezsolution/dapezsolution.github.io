2. What was sold, and where the money went

Anthropic sold 980 million Class A shares at $83: 956,351,354 new shares for the company and 23,648,646 sold by the seven co-founders (3,378,378 each). Demand ran at roughly six times the shares on offer, so the company raised the price above the range and sold 155 million more shares than planned. Up to 20% of the deal was set aside for retail investors.

  • To Anthropic: about $79.4bn from new shares, plus $26bn of new cash from the private placement, so roughly $105bn of fresh capital before fees (our arithmetic).
  • To the co-founders: about $280m each, $1.96bn together. They kept the rest.
  • Private placement: Google about $10bn, Nvidia about $10bn, AMD $2.5bn, and Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC the rest. These buyers receive non-voting Class N shares, not the Class A shares sold to the public. AMD's $2.5bn also counts against its earlier $5bn equity commitment tied to GPU deliveries.

Impact on the company

  • Cash for compute. The S-1 shows $265.6bn of fixed or minimum-guaranteed commitments at 30 June 2026, almost all for compute and data centres, and at least $417bn of future minimum infrastructure payments overall. The IPO money mainly funds this bill.
  • Dilution. About 1.27bn new shares (public offering + placement) against roughly 13bn shares outstanding means existing holders were diluted by about 10% (our arithmetic). The Public Benefit Fund already holds 846.8m Class N shares (5% fully diluted), and the board can issue up to 2% more each time the share price rises 50% above the previous milestone.
  • Control does not change. The seven co-founders hold 50.1% of voting power through a Class F share, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust (Class T) elects a majority of the board. Public shareholders get the economics, not control.

How many millionaires and billionaires?

  • 7 co-founders, including CEO Dario Amodei and President Daniela Amodei, are each worth about $20bn at the IPO price, in the same league as Rupert Murdoch's family. They have pledged 80% of their wealth to charity.
  • About 2,800 employees hold at least $1m of vested equity.
  • 400 to 600 employees could hold stakes worth more than $100m, and investors expect several more employee billionaires.
  • Average employee equity: about $44m at $83 (CNBC).
  • Forbes counts ten existing billionaires who became a combined $49bn richer on the listing.

Caveat: this is paper wealth. Employees could sell only up to 10% from day one; the rest unlocks in stages to mid-2027, and taxes apply. If the share price falls, so does this wealth.

Investors then and now

  • Early and venture backers: Spark Capital, Lightspeed, Menlo Ventures (stake worth more than $14bn), ICONIQ (about $17bn), Sequoia, Coatue, GIC, General Catalyst, Fidelity, D1, Founders Fund, D.E. Shaw, MGX, Altimeter, Dragoneer, Greenoaks, and Ashton Kutcher's Sound Ventures (about 0.5%, roughly $7bn). No venture fund is a 5% holder.
  • The investor that missed out: the FTX bankruptcy estate sold its Anthropic shares in 2024; at the IPO price they would be worth about $37bn.
  • New on IPO day: Google, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC through the $26bn placement, plus institutional and retail buyers of the 980m public shares.
  • Strategic web: Amazon, Google and Microsoft are investors, suppliers, sales channels and competitors at the same time.

What the comparison shows

  • Anthropic is the biggest raise, but its +17% pop is middle of the pack, very close to SpaceX (+19%) and Google 2004 (+18%). It opened 34% higher, about three times SpaceX's opening pop, then gave back about 13% from the first trade.
  • The first minute alone traded about $15.6bn of stock, almost twice SpaceX's first minute.
  • History is a warning: of the six biggest tech IPOs since Facebook, five were below their first-day close six months later. The three largest US IPOs (SpaceX, Alibaba, Visa) all rose 19–38% on day one, peaked within two months, then fell about 50% from those peaks. SpaceX now trades below its $135 offer price.
  • In 2026, 37 of 52 US IPOs worth $1bn or more closed below their offer price by 16 November.

5. Impact on Nasdaq, the S&P 500 and related stocks

  • Day one was a "sell the news" session for the rest of the market. The Nasdaq Composite rose as much as 1.9% at the open, then reversed. It closed −2.25% at 23,879; the S&P 500 fell 2.02% to 7,175.83, its sixth straight loss and longest losing streak since 2024. QQQ (Nasdaq-100) fell 2.19%, the Russell 2000 2.18%.
  • Anthropic's partners fell hardest: Amazon −3.87%, Alphabet −3.46%, Nvidia −3.30% (after being up 4.7% early, a day after strong earnings), Micron −2.10%, Broadcom −2.09%, Microsoft −1.97%. Investors appear to have sold the indirect "Anthropic proxies" once the real stock was available.
  • Nasdaq-100: under the fast-entry rule adopted in 2026, ANT is expected to join around 21 December. Index funds will then have to buy it, and the weight of every other member falls slightly. SpaceX's experience shows inclusion is a flow event, not a guarantee of gains.
  • S&P 500: not yet. S&P keeps its seasoning and GAAP profitability rules, and Anthropic reports large net losses. Its effect on the S&P 500 is indirect, through Amazon, Alphabet, Nvidia, Broadcom and Microsoft.
  • Russell 1000: possible fast entry as early as December.

6. Risks

Company risks (from the S-1)

  1. Compute bill. Hundreds of billions in non-cancellable commitments. Too little capacity limits growth; commitments that outrun demand leave Anthropic paying for unused capacity. Management admits it has underestimated compute needs and struggled to forecast revenue.
  2. "Friend and foe" suppliers. More than three-fifths of compute commitments are with Amazon, Google and Microsoft, which also build rival models and can ration capacity.
  3. Customer concentration. The top five customers produced about 25% of revenue in the nine months to 30 September 2026.
  4. Losses and accounting. Net loss of $80.7bn in H1 2026, about $72bn of it non-cash fair-value charges. Q3 revenue was $16.8bn (+45% on Q2) and adjusted profit positive, but GAAP losses keep it out of the S&P 500.
  5. US government dispute. The Department of War supply-chain-risk designation and the federal-use directive are still being litigated. Government sales are only about 1% of revenue, but the reputational and regulatory risk is wider.
  6. Governance. Founder voting control (50.1%), the Long-Term Benefit Trust's power over the board, and built-in Public Benefit Fund dilution. Public shareholders have little say.
  7. AI safety and liability. The S-1 warns that a catastrophic AI incident could exceed insurance cover.
  8. Short track record. About five years of operations and a very short history of today's revenue scale.

Stock and market risks

  1. Valuation. At $1.76trn fully diluted the stock prices in years of very fast growth. Any slowdown can re-rate it sharply.
  2. Lock-up supply. About 15.5bn shares sit under lock-up or market-standoff terms. Staged releases (10% now, 35% after FY2026 results, most by mid-May 2027, founders up to 365 days) add supply.
  3. Index-flow reversal. Fast money often buys early and sells into the forced index buying around inclusion.
  4. AI-trade sentiment. The debut came during a six-day S&P 500 losing streak and talk of an AI bubble. A wider tech sell-off would hit ANT.
  5. Post-IPO history. The largest IPOs, including SpaceX this year, have tended to fall well below their early highs within a year.

7. What to watch next

  • Day-two and first-week trading against the $83 IPO price (the key support level)
  • Whether underwriters exercise the 147m-share option (a sign of after-market demand)
  • Nasdaq-100 entry around 21 December
  • Full-year 2026 results, the first as a public company, and the next 35% employee unlock
  • Compute-commitment growth vs revenue growth each quarter
  • The appeal in the Department of War case

Bottom line: Anthropic raised more money than any company in history and closed day one up 17%, yet the wider market fell more than 2% the same day. The listing turned seven founders into $20bn fortunes and thousands of staff into millionaires on paper. Whether that wealth lasts depends on revenue outrunning a compute bill measured in hundreds of billions.
